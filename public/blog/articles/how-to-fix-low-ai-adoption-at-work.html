How to Fix Low AI Adoption at Work: A Practical Guide for Managers

Learn how to fix low AI adoption at work by identifying access, workflow, and skill barriers. Includes practical examples, a two-week pilot, and measurable success criteria.

17 November 2026 12 min read

Your employees have completed their AI training.

They understand the basics, know how to write prompts, and have seen examples of AI supporting everyday tasks.

But when you look at how the team actually works, very little has changed.

Reports are still drafted manually. Approved AI tools remain unused. Employees return to familiar processes.

The natural response might be to organize another training session.

But low AI adoption is not always a training problem.

Sometimes employees cannot access the approved tool. Sometimes internal procedures prevent them from using it. And sometimes they can generate an AI-assisted result but lack the skills to verify its accuracy.

These situations may look similar from a manager’s perspective, but they require different interventions.

This guide explains how to diagnose three common AI adoption barriers, select an appropriate solution, and test whether your intervention improves actual workplace performance.

Why Employees May Not Use AI After Training

Completing a course and applying what was learned are different outcomes.

An employee might demonstrate strong AI knowledge during training but encounter an obstacle when returning to work.

Consider a fictional operations team responsible for preparing a weekly report.

Every Friday, employees transform information from a spreadsheet into a short summary.

The organization has introduced an approved AI tool and provided relevant training.

Yet the team continues preparing reports manually.

Before assigning more training, a manager should observe how the task is completed.

Ask one employee to show the previous report and explain each step.

Then identify where the process breaks down.

Three particularly useful areas to investigate are:

  1. Access: Can the employee open and use the approved AI tool?
  2. Workflow: Do existing procedures support the intended AI-assisted process?
  3. Verification: Can the employee check the AI-generated result against reliable source information?

The answers help determine what should happen next.

Step 1: Define the Workplace Task Before Choosing a Solution

A common mistake is trying to improve AI adoption without defining what successful application looks like.

For example, a manager might tell employees:

“Start using AI more often.”

But this instruction does not identify a specific task, expected output, or quality standard.

A better approach is to define the target behavior.

Use a Three-Part Instruction

Structure the instruction around:

Action + Output + Quality Requirement

For the weekly reporting example:

Action: Draft.

Output: A weekly summary.

Quality requirement: Verify important figures against the source spreadsheet and submit the draft for human review.

The resulting instruction becomes:

“Draft the weekly summary using the approved AI tool, verify the figures against the source, and send the result for review.”

This is more useful than simply asking employees to use AI.

It defines what the employee should produce and what conditions must be met before the task is complete.

The task should also be compatible with the organization’s data handling rules and approved AI tools.

Step 2: Fix Access Barriers Before Assigning More Training

Imagine an employee who understands the reporting task and knows how to use the AI tool.

They open the approved link.

The screen displays:

Access denied.

The employee cannot continue.

In this situation, another prompting course will not solve the immediate problem.

The first intervention should address tool access.

How to Diagnose an Access Barrier

Ask the employee to demonstrate the sign-in process.

Observe whether they can:

  • Open the approved tool.
  • Authenticate successfully.
  • Access the required workspace.
  • Use the features necessary for the assigned task.

If the employee cannot enter the workspace, involve the appropriate IT or administrative team.

What to Do Next

Follow the organization’s normal access request process.

Confirm the employee’s account, permissions, and entitlement to use the tool.

Assign an owner to the issue.

Once the request has been resolved, ask the employee to repeat the sign-in independently.

Do not treat the IT ticket being closed as sufficient evidence.

The meaningful result is that the employee can actually access the approved workspace.

After access is restored, ask them to attempt the reporting task again.

If they still cannot complete it, investigate the next barrier.

Step 3: Remove Workflow Barriers That Prevent AI Use

Now consider a second employee.

They can access the approved AI tool and understand how to create a report draft.

However, the team’s reporting checklist still contains this instruction:

“Draft the entire report manually.”

The employee follows the existing procedure.

AI training has introduced a new capability, but the operational workflow has not changed.

This is primarily a process problem.

Identify the Conflicting Instruction

Review the documents and procedures employees use to complete the task.

Look for:

  • Instructions requiring manual work.
  • Approval steps that do not accommodate AI-assisted drafts.
  • Missing guidance about approved AI tools.
  • Unclear responsibilities for reviewing outputs.
  • Requirements that conflict with the proposed workflow.

The objective is not to remove safeguards.

It is to determine whether the process can safely accommodate the new capability.

Update One Step With the Process Owner

If the organization approves AI assistance for the task, a revised checklist might say:

  1. Prepare the first draft using the approved AI tool.
  2. Check important figures against the source.
  3. Include the relevant source references.
  4. Submit the draft for human review.

The process owner should approve the change before employees begin using the revised procedure.

Test the Updated Workflow

Start with one report rather than changing the entire department’s process immediately.

Ask the reviewer:

  • Was the report usable?
  • Were the source figures identifiable?
  • What corrections were necessary?
  • Did the revised handoff work as intended?

This helps identify whether the updated procedure improves the task or introduces new difficulties.

Step 4: Address Verification Skills When Employees Miss AI Errors

A third employee has access to the tool and follows the updated workflow.

They produce a report using AI.

However, the draft states:

Total orders: 86

The source spreadsheet states:

Total orders: 68

The employee does not notice the discrepancy.

This is a different problem.

Access works.

The workflow permits AI assistance.

But the employee has not demonstrated the ability to verify the result.

Why AI Output Verification Matters

AI-generated content can contain inaccurate numbers, unsupported statements, or incorrect interpretations.

Employees should not assume that a plausible-looking result is correct.

For tasks involving operational reporting, financial information, or important business decisions, verification is essential.

Create a Focused Verification Exercise

Provide the employee with a fictional report containing a deliberate numerical mismatch.

Give them the corresponding source spreadsheet.

Ask them to:

  1. Identify the incorrect figure.
  2. Locate the correct value in the source.
  3. Correct the draft.
  4. Record the source reference for the reviewer.

The exercise should evaluate whether the employee can perform these steps independently.

Reassess With a New Example

If the employee misses the error, demonstrate the comparison process.

Then provide a fresh exercise.

For example:

AI draft: 42 returns.

Source document: 24 returns.

The employee should locate the relevant source row, correct the number, and explain where the verified value came from.

This provides observable evidence of the specific skill being developed.

It is more targeted than repeating a general AI fundamentals course.

Step 5: Match Each Barrier to the Right Intervention

Once the manager understands the obstacle, selecting an intervention becomes easier.

Observed Problem Likely Barrier First Intervention
Employee receives an access-denied message Tool access Verify permissions and retest
Employee follows a manual-only procedure Workflow Review and update the approved process
Employee misses incorrect AI-generated figures Verification skill Provide targeted practice and reassessment
Employee still cannot complete the task after access is restored Further investigation needed Observe the next point of failure

This distinction helps organizations avoid unnecessary training.

The objective is not to provide more learning activities. It is to remove the barrier preventing effective work.

Step 6: Run a Two-Week AI Adoption Experiment

After identifying the problem, test one intervention before making broader changes.

A small pilot can help determine whether the proposed solution is useful.

Week 1: Establish a Baseline

Choose a small group of employees performing comparable work.

Ask them to complete a report using the current process.

Record three outcomes:

Measure Question
Task completion Did the employee finish the report?
Verification Did the employee verify the figures?
Independence Did the employee need help?

Use simple yes-or-no responses and add short notes when necessary.

For a more detailed evaluation, you can also record task duration and the number of corrections required.

However, avoid introducing more measurements than the pilot can reasonably support.

Introduce One Change

Select the intervention that corresponds to the observed barrier.

For example:

  • Resolve an access issue.
  • Update one reporting instruction.
  • Introduce a focused verification exercise.

Document who is responsible for the change and when it takes effect.

Changing several factors simultaneously can make it harder to understand what contributed to the result.

Week 2: Repeat a Comparable Task

Ask the same employees to complete a similar report.

Use the same success criteria.

Record the same measures.

This creates a basic before-and-after comparison.

Example: Interpreting the Results

Imagine a pilot involving five employees.

During the first week, two employees successfully verify the report figures.

During the second week, four employees do so.

Measure Week 1 Week 2
Employees verifying figures 2 of 5 4 of 5
Verification rate 40% 80%

This is an illustrative example, not a real customer result.

The improvement is encouraging, but it does not establish that the intervention caused the change.

The sample is small, and factors such as task difficulty or additional support may influence the outcome.

The next step is to investigate whether employees can repeat the result independently.

Step 7: Measure Independent Performance, Not Just Successful Attempts

Suppose four employees verify the figures correctly during the second week.

That looks promising.

But imagine three of them needed someone to point out the correct spreadsheet row.

The result tells a more nuanced story.

The team can complete the verification with support, but independent performance is not yet consistent.

This distinction matters.

An employee completing a task with continuous assistance is not demonstrating the same capability as someone completing it independently.

Conduct a Follow-Up Assessment

Provide a new report with a comparable verification challenge.

Keep the same quality standard.

Ask employees to complete the task without prompts.

Observe whether they can:

  • Locate the relevant source information.
  • Identify discrepancies.
  • Correct the output.
  • Explain their verification process.
  • Complete the task without unnecessary assistance.

Where possible, repeat the assessment during normal work rather than relying exclusively on a supervised exercise.

This provides a better indication of whether the capability is transferring into everyday responsibilities.

Step 8: Document the Intervention and Its Owner

A useful improvement process needs clear accountability.

After selecting the intervention, document five details:

Task

Barrier

Action

Owner

Retest Date

For example:

Field Example
Task Weekly operations report
Barrier Checklist requires manual drafting
Action Permit an AI-assisted first draft with source checks and human review
Owner Operations Lead
Retest Date Next Friday

This simple record makes the next action explicit.

It also helps prevent the issue from disappearing into a general discussion about AI adoption.

Common Mistakes When Trying to Improve AI Adoption

Assigning Training Before Observing the Problem

Employees may already have the necessary skills.

First identify the obstacle.

Assuming Tool Access Means Employees Can Use the Tool

An active account does not guarantee that employees understand the workflow or can complete the task.

Updating Processes Without Reviewing Risks

AI-assisted workflows still need appropriate safeguards, source verification, and human oversight.

Measuring Only Whether AI Was Used

Using AI is not automatically an improvement.

The output must meet the required quality standard.

Changing Too Many Things at Once

A focused pilot makes it easier to interpret results and identify remaining problems.

Confusing Assisted Performance With Independent Capability

Employees may complete a task successfully when coached but struggle without support.

Reassessment should account for this difference.

When Should Companies Expand an AI Adoption Pilot?

A pilot should not be expanded simply because employees report enjoying the new tool.

Before introducing the workflow to a larger group, evaluate whether:

  • Employees can complete the task independently.
  • Outputs meet established quality standards.
  • Important information is verified.
  • The process follows organizational policies.
  • Review responsibilities are clear.
  • The workflow provides a meaningful operational benefit.

If these conditions are not met, the organization may need to revise the intervention or continue testing.

Sometimes the correct decision is not to expand AI use.

A successful AI adoption strategy should distinguish useful applications from those that create additional risk or complexity.

Final Thoughts: Fix the Actual Barrier

When employees do not use AI after training, it is tempting to assume that they need more education.

But the real obstacle may be technical, operational, or related to a specific capability.

A manager who observes the work can distinguish these situations.

If the tool is inaccessible, resolve access.

If the process prevents AI use, review the workflow.

If the employee cannot verify the result, provide focused skill development.

Then test the intervention using a comparable task and clear success criteria.

Effective AI adoption begins with understanding what prevents employees from applying their skills.

The next step is to make one meaningful change and determine whether it improves the work.
