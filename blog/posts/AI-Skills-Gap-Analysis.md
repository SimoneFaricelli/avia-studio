Companies are investing in artificial intelligence, but introducing new tools does not automatically mean employees have the skills to use them effectively.

Some employees already use AI regularly.

Others understand the fundamentals but struggle to apply them to their jobs.

And some may have limited experience with AI altogether.

This creates an important challenge for HR managers, Learning and Development teams and business leaders:

**How do you identify which AI skills employees actually need to develop first?**

The answer starts with an **AI skills gap analysis**.

Rather than assigning the same training to everyone, organizations can compare existing employee capabilities with the skills required for specific roles.

This makes it possible to identify meaningful gaps, prioritize training and allocate learning resources more effectively.

In this guide, we'll walk through a practical framework for conducting an AI skills gap analysis, including a scoring method, worked examples and a way to turn the results into targeted learning plans.

## What Is an AI Skills Gap Analysis?

An AI skills gap analysis is a structured process for identifying the difference between an employee's current AI capabilities and the capabilities required for their work.

It answers three questions:

1. What AI skills does the employee currently demonstrate?
2. What AI skills does their role require?
3. Which differences should the organization address through training?

The goal is not to make every employee an AI expert.

It is to ensure that employees develop the capabilities necessary to use AI appropriately and effectively in their roles.

For example, an HR professional may need strong skills in evaluating AI-generated information and handling sensitive data responsibly.

A salesperson may need more experience applying AI to research, meeting preparation and communication.

Both employees may use similar AI tools, but their learning priorities can differ significantly.

## Why AI Skills Gaps Matter for Companies

Traditional corporate training often begins with a course.

A company selects an AI training program, assigns it to employees and measures completion.

But this approach can overlook important differences in existing skills.

Consider a fictional company with 50 employees.

Some employees already understand prompting.

Others struggle to evaluate AI-generated outputs.

Some understand AI limitations but cannot identify relevant workplace applications.

If everyone receives the same introductory course, the organization may spend time teaching capabilities employees already possess while leaving other important gaps unresolved.

A skills gap analysis provides a more useful starting point.

Instead of asking:

**"Which AI course should we give everyone?"**

the organization asks:

**"Which capabilities are missing, and where do they matter most?"**

## Step 1: Define the AI Skills Your Workforce Needs

Before assessing employees, organizations need to define which AI capabilities are relevant.

Avoid creating an unnecessarily complicated framework.

Start with a manageable set of skills.

For example:

### AI Fundamentals

Understanding what generative AI can and cannot do.

### Prompting

Communicating tasks, context and constraints clearly to an AI system.

### Output Evaluation

Reviewing AI-generated information, identifying errors and verifying important claims.

### Responsible AI Use

Understanding organizational policies, confidentiality requirements and appropriate human oversight.

### Practical Application

Using AI to support realistic tasks related to an employee's responsibilities.

### Workflow Integration

Identifying where AI can appropriately support recurring professional activities.

These six dimensions provide a useful starting point.

Organizations can add more specialized capabilities where necessary.

The important principle is to assess skills that matter for actual work rather than knowledge that is merely easy to test.

## Step 2: Establish Required Skill Levels by Role

Not every employee needs the same level of proficiency.

A practical approach is to define required skill levels using a consistent scale.

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| 0 | No capability required or demonstrated |
| 1 | Basic understanding |
| 2 | Independent application in routine tasks |
| 3 | Proficient application in more complex situations |

The distinction between a capability not being required and an employee not demonstrating it should be recorded separately.

A target level of zero means the skill is not currently required for that role.

A current level of zero means the employee has not demonstrated the skill.

These are different situations.

### Example: Required Skills by Department

The following table is an illustrative example, not a universal industry standard.

| Skill | Sales | HR | Finance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| AI Fundamentals | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| Prompting | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| Output Evaluation | 2 | 3 | 3 |
| Responsible AI Use | 2 | 3 | 3 |
| Practical Application | 3 | 2 | 2 |
| Workflow Integration | 2 | 2 | 2 |

These targets should be adjusted to actual responsibilities, company policies and the AI tools employees are permitted to use.

For example, a finance employee working extensively with AI-assisted analysis may need different capabilities from another employee in the same department.

**Job titles provide context, but actual responsibilities should determine training requirements.**

## Step 3: Assess Employees' Current AI Skills

Once required capabilities have been defined, the organization needs to establish current proficiency.

There are several ways to gather evidence.

### Knowledge Assessments

Short quizzes can evaluate AI fundamentals, basic prompting concepts and awareness of limitations.

### Practical Exercises

Employees can demonstrate how they apply AI to realistic professional tasks.

For example, they might review an AI-generated summary and identify unsupported claims.

### Scenario-Based Questions

Employees can explain how they would approach situations involving confidential information, output verification or human oversight.

### Work Samples

Where appropriate and permitted, employees can demonstrate relevant skills using fictional or approved work materials.

### Employee Self-Assessments

Self-assessments can provide useful information about confidence and perceived skill gaps.

However, self-reported confidence should not be treated as equivalent to demonstrated competence.

For more reliable results, combine knowledge checks with practical evidence.

## Step 4: Calculate the AI Skills Gap

After collecting assessment results, compare current proficiency with required proficiency.

A simple formula is:

**Skill Gap = MAX(0, Required Level âˆ’ Current Level)**

This produces a non-negative gap score.

A score of zero means the employee meets or exceeds the required level.

A positive score indicates a potential training need.

### Worked Example

Consider Alex, a fictional sales employee.

| AI Skill | Required | Current | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| AI Fundamentals | 2 | 2 | 0 |
| Prompting | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| Output Evaluation | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| Responsible AI Use | 2 | 2 | 0 |
| Practical Application | 3 | 1 | 2 |
| Workflow Integration | 2 | 1 | 1 |

Alex's largest gap is in practical application.

This suggests that assigning another general introduction to AI may not be the most useful next step.

Instead, training could focus on applying AI to relevant sales activities.

For example:

- Preparing for a prospect meeting.
- Researching a fictional customer account.
- Structuring discovery questions.
- Reviewing and improving AI-generated communication.

Alex also needs development in prompting, output evaluation and workflow integration.

The skills gap analysis helps make these priorities visible.

## Step 5: Prioritize the Most Important Gaps

Not every gap should receive the same priority.

A gap of two points in an irrelevant capability may matter less than a one-point gap in a critical skill.

Companies should therefore consider both:

**Gap size**

and:

**Importance to the employee's role**

A simple illustrative prioritization method is:

**Training Priority Score = Skill Gap Ã— Role Importance**

Assign role importance using a scale from 1 to 3:

- 1 = Useful but not essential.
- 2 = Important for recurring work.
- 3 = Critical for the role.

### Example

| Skill | Gap | Importance | Priority |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Prompting | 1 | 2 | 2 |
| Output Evaluation | 1 | 3 | 3 |
| Practical Application | 2 | 3 | 6 |
| Workflow Integration | 1 | 2 | 2 |

In this example, practical application receives the highest priority.

Output evaluation comes next.

This scoring system is a planning aid, not a scientifically validated measure of training impact.

Organizations should also apply minimum competency requirements for safety-critical or policy-sensitive capabilities.

A low numerical priority must never override an important confidentiality, security or responsible-use requirement.

## Step 6: Turn Skill Gaps Into Targeted Training

A skills gap analysis is only useful when it influences what employees learn next.

Consider three fictional employees.

### Employee A: Sales

**Main gap:** Practical AI application.

**Training focus:** Applying AI to prospect research and meeting preparation.

**Practice task:** Use approved AI tools to prepare a structured briefing for a fictional sales meeting.

**Success criterion:** The employee produces a relevant, accurate briefing and can explain how they verified the information.

### Employee B: Human Resources

**Main gap:** Output evaluation.

**Training focus:** Reviewing AI-generated summaries and identifying unsupported claims.

**Practice task:** Compare a generated summary with a fictional internal policy document.

**Success criterion:** The employee identifies important inaccuracies and corrects them without introducing new errors.

### Employee C: Finance

**Main gap:** Workflow integration.

**Training focus:** Identifying appropriate AI applications in recurring financial information workflows.

**Practice task:** Map a fictional reporting process and identify where approved AI assistance could be useful.

**Success criterion:** The employee proposes an appropriate workflow, verification steps and clear human responsibilities.

These employees do not need identical training.

Their learning plans should reflect their actual gaps.

## Step 7: Build a Personalized AI Training Plan

Once training priorities are established, organizations can create a structured learning plan.

A practical plan should contain:

- Employee role and responsibilities.
- Current skill levels.
- Required skill levels.
- Priority gaps.
- Relevant learning activities.
- Practical exercises.
- Assessment criteria.
- Reassessment date.

A simple spreadsheet is sufficient to get started.

For example:

| Employee | Priority Gap | Learning Activity | Practical Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alex | Practical Application | AI for Sales Research | Prepare a verified prospect briefing |
| Jordan | Output Evaluation | AI Output Verification | Correct an inaccurate policy summary |
| Taylor | Workflow Integration | AI-Assisted Reporting | Design a reviewed reporting workflow |

The purpose is not to create the most complicated training system possible.

It is to make the next learning step clear and relevant.

## Step 8: Reassess Skills After Training

Training completion does not automatically mean a skill gap has been closed.

After employees complete relevant learning activities, organizations should evaluate whether their demonstrated capabilities have improved.

For example:

**Before training:**

An employee struggles to identify unsupported claims in an AI-generated summary.

**After training:**

The employee completes a comparable exercise using a different source document.

If they can identify and correct the errors accurately, the organization has evidence of improved capability.

However, improved assessment performance does not automatically prove workplace adoption.

That requires additional observation.

Companies may therefore want to distinguish:

**Skills Development**

from:

**Workplace Application**

The first measures whether employees have learned.

The second examines whether they use those capabilities appropriately in their work.

## Common AI Skills Gap Analysis Mistakes

### Using the Same Target Levels for Everyone

Different roles require different capabilities.

A universal proficiency target may create unnecessary training.

### Relying Only on Self-Reported Skills

Confidence does not always reflect demonstrated competence.

Practical assessments can provide stronger evidence.

### Treating All Gaps as Equally Important

Training should reflect business relevance and risk, not only numerical differences.

### Ignoring Responsible AI Use

Organizations should not overlook confidentiality, security, accuracy or human oversight requirements.

### Creating Assessments Without Changing Training

Collecting employee scores has limited value if everyone receives the same course afterward.

### Measuring Only Completion

Completing a training module does not necessarily mean the underlying skill has improved.

## How Often Should Companies Conduct an AI Skills Gap Analysis?

There is no universal frequency that works for every organization.

A practical approach is to conduct an initial analysis before introducing a major training program.

Organizations can then reassess employees after relevant learning activities and when significant changes occur.

These changes might include:

- New AI tools.
- New employee responsibilities.
- Updated organizational policies.
- New workplace use cases.
- Changes in required capabilities.

The objective is to maintain a useful understanding of workforce skills without creating unnecessary administrative work.

## From AI Skills Gaps to Workforce AI Readiness

An AI skills gap analysis is not simply an exercise in scoring employees.

It is a way to make better decisions about training.

Instead of assigning courses based on assumptions, organizations can use evidence to understand:

**What employees already know.**

**What their roles require.**

**Which skills need attention.**

**What training should happen next.**

This creates a clearer connection between workforce development and practical AI adoption.

Companies do not need every employee to develop identical AI expertise.

They need employees with the capabilities required to use AI appropriately in their own work.

That begins with identifying the right gaps.

And then building training that addresses them.
