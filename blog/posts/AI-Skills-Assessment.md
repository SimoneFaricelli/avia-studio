Artificial intelligence is entering the workplace faster than employees are developing the skills to use it.

Inside the same company, one employee may use AI every day while another may barely know where to begin.

Some employees understand prompting but struggle to identify useful applications.

Others experiment constantly but do not understand the limitations of AI-generated outputs.

This creates a problem for organizations trying to build effective AI training programs.

If employees start from different levels, giving everyone the same course is unlikely to produce the same results.

Before deciding what employees need to learn, companies need to understand **what they already know**.

That is where an **AI skills assessment** becomes useful.

## What is an AI skills assessment?

An AI skills assessment is a structured evaluation designed to understand an employee's current knowledge, capabilities and confidence when working with artificial intelligence.

The objective is not simply to determine whether someone understands what AI is.

A useful assessment can explore several dimensions of AI capability, including:

- AI fundamentals;
- prompting skills;
- understanding of AI limitations;
- ability to evaluate AI-generated outputs;
- practical application;
- responsible AI use;
- ability to identify relevant workplace use cases;
- experience integrating AI into existing workflows.

The results can provide organizations with a clearer picture of where employees currently stand.

Instead of assuming everyone needs the same training, companies can begin from actual skill gaps.

## Why companies need to understand existing AI skills

Imagine two employees joining the same corporate AI training program.

The first already uses generative AI every day.

They understand prompting, experiment with different workflows and know how to evaluate AI-generated responses.

The second has only used an AI assistant a few times.

If both employees receive the same introductory course, the learning experience may be inefficient for both.

The experienced employee spends time repeating concepts they already understand.

The beginner may struggle if the course progresses too quickly.

The problem is not necessarily the quality of the course.

The problem is the assumption that:

**every employee starts from the same place.**

An AI skills assessment helps challenge that assumption.

## What should an AI skills assessment measure?

AI capability is broader than knowing how to write a prompt.

Companies should consider multiple dimensions when assessing employees.

## 1. AI fundamentals

Employees should have a basic understanding of what modern AI systems can and cannot do.

This may include understanding:

- what generative AI is;
- how AI-generated outputs should be interpreted;
- why AI can produce incorrect information;
- why human review remains important;
- where AI may or may not be appropriate.

Employees do not need to become machine learning engineers.

But they should understand enough to use AI with appropriate judgment.

## 2. Prompting skills

Prompting is one of the most visible AI skills.

An assessment can explore whether employees understand how to:

- provide clear instructions;
- add relevant context;
- define desired outputs;
- refine an initial request;
- improve responses through iteration.

However, prompting should not become the entire assessment.

Knowing how to write a sophisticated prompt is useful.

Knowing **when that prompt can improve real work** is even more important.

## 3. Ability to evaluate AI outputs

AI literacy also requires critical thinking.

Employees need to understand that AI-generated responses should not automatically be treated as correct.

A skills assessment can explore whether employees know how to:

- verify important information;
- identify questionable outputs;
- recognize uncertainty;
- review generated content;
- maintain appropriate human oversight.

This becomes particularly important when AI is used for professional activities.

## 4. Ability to identify relevant AI use cases

One of the most important capabilities is recognizing where AI can actually help.

An employee may understand AI technically while still struggling to connect it to their role.

For example, a salesperson might identify opportunities involving:

- prospect research;
- meeting preparation;
- account information;
- written communication.

Someone working in HR may identify different applications:

- organizing information;
- summarizing documents;
- preparing internal communication;
- supporting documentation workflows.

The important skill is not simply knowing that AI exists.

It is understanding:

**“Where could this improve the work I already do?”**

## 5. Practical AI application

There is a significant difference between theoretical knowledge and practical capability.

An employee may know what prompting is but struggle when asked to apply AI to a realistic work task.

Practical assessments can therefore be valuable.

Instead of asking only:

**“What is generative AI?”**

an assessment might evaluate whether an employee can use AI appropriately to solve a relevant professional problem.

This provides a better indication of whether knowledge can translate into action.

## 6. Responsible AI use

Effective AI training should also consider responsible use.

Employees should understand relevant organizational policies and recognize situations where additional caution is required.

Depending on the company and role, this can include awareness around:

- sensitive information;
- confidential data;
- accuracy;
- human oversight;
- appropriate tool usage;
- organizational AI policies.

The objective is not to make employees afraid of using AI.

It is to help them use it with appropriate judgment.

## 7. Workflow integration

A more advanced level of AI capability involves integrating AI into recurring work.

There is a progression between:

**knowing AI → experimenting with AI → applying AI → integrating AI**

An employee at the experimentation stage may occasionally open an AI tool and try different prompts.

An employee at the integration stage may understand where AI fits into a recurring workflow and use it consistently when appropriate.

An assessment can help identify where employees currently sit on this progression.

## AI skill levels should not be reduced to beginner or expert

It can be tempting to classify employees simply as:

**Beginner**

**Intermediate**

**Advanced**

These categories are useful, but they can hide important differences.

An employee might have:

- advanced prompting skills;
- intermediate AI knowledge;
- limited understanding of responsible use;
- strong practical application skills.

Another employee could have exactly the opposite profile.

A more useful approach is to create an **AI skill profile** rather than relying on a single score.

For example:

**AI Fundamentals: 80%**

**Prompting: 70%**

**Output Evaluation: 55%**

**Practical Application: 40%**

**Responsible AI Use: 75%**

This makes skill gaps easier to identify.

It can also make the next step — personalized training — more precise.

## From AI skills assessment to personalized training

Assessment should not be the end of the process.

Its real value appears when the results influence what happens next.

Instead of:

**Employee → Generic AI Course**

companies can move toward:

**Employee → AI Skills Assessment → Skill Gaps → Personalized Learning Path**

Consider two employees.

Employee A already understands AI fundamentals but needs help applying AI to their role.

Employee B understands relevant workplace use cases but needs stronger prompting and output evaluation skills.

Giving both employees identical training wastes information the organization already has.

Personalized learning can focus on the capabilities each employee actually needs.

## Role should be considered alongside skill level

Skill level alone is not enough.

Two employees with identical AI knowledge may still need completely different training because they perform different jobs.

Consider:

**Sales**

and:

**Finance**

Both employees may have intermediate AI skills.

But the applications relevant to their work can differ significantly.

This means a stronger training model can combine:

**Current AI Skills + Role + Responsibilities**

to determine:

**Relevant Learning Path**

The result is training that considers both what an employee knows and what they actually do.

## How companies can run an AI skills assessment

Organizations can approach AI skills assessment through a simple process.

### 1. Define the capabilities that matter

Identify the AI skills employees need based on the organization's objectives.

Avoid assessing knowledge simply because it is easy to test.

Focus on capabilities that employees may actually need.

### 2. Establish a baseline

Assess employees before assigning training.

This creates a starting point for future comparison.

### 3. Combine knowledge with practical questions

Traditional multiple-choice questions can measure understanding.

Practical scenarios can help measure application.

Using both can provide a more complete picture.

### 4. Consider employee roles

Not every employee needs to demonstrate exactly the same AI capabilities.

Assessment can include both common AI fundamentals and role-relevant skills.

### 5. Identify individual skill gaps

Avoid looking only at the company average.

Averages can hide major differences between employees.

Individual skill profiles provide more useful information for training.

### 6. Connect results to learning

Use assessment results to determine what employees should learn next.

The assessment should influence the training experience.

### 7. Reassess over time

AI skills are not static.

Employees learn.

AI tools evolve.

New use cases emerge.

Periodic reassessment can help organizations understand how workforce capabilities are developing.

## How AI skills assessments can support enterprise AI adoption

AI adoption depends partly on whether employees have the capabilities required to use artificial intelligence effectively.

Without visibility into those capabilities, companies may struggle to understand why adoption is slow.

Low AI usage could result from:

- insufficient knowledge;
- poor prompting skills;
- lack of confidence;
- unclear use cases;
- irrelevant training;
- uncertainty about responsible use.

An assessment helps make these gaps more visible.

Organizations can then respond with more targeted learning rather than simply providing additional generic training.

## Avoid turning AI skills assessment into another checkbox

There is one important risk.

Companies should not replace:

**“Everyone completed the course.”**

with:

**“Everyone completed the assessment.”**

The purpose of an AI skills assessment is not to create another completion metric.

It should help organizations make better decisions about workforce development.

The useful questions are:

**What does this employee already know?**

**Where are their skill gaps?**

**What capabilities matter for their role?**

**What should they learn next?**

When assessments answer those questions, they become part of a broader AI adoption strategy rather than another administrative exercise.

## Start with what employees actually need

Companies do not need every employee to become an AI expert.

They need employees who can use artificial intelligence appropriately where it creates value.

That requires understanding both:

**what employees do**

and:

**what employees already know.**

AI skills assessments provide a starting point.

From there, organizations can build training that is more relevant, more personalized and more closely connected to actual work.

Instead of asking:

**“Which AI course should we give everyone?”**

the better question may be:

**“What does each employee need to learn next?”**
